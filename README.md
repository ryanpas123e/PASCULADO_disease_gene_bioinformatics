The selected MSH2 transcript, ENST00000233146.7, contains 16 exons. The UCSC tooltip identifies the transcript and shows "Exon 1 of 16." Multiple horizontal transcript models are also visible in the GENCODE and RefSeq tracks, indicating that MSH2 has multiple transcript/isoform models.

An exon is a region of a gene that remains in the mature RNA after RNA splicing. An intron is a region between exons that is removed from the pre-mRNA during RNA splicing.

In the MSH2 gene, the introns generally appear longer than the exons. The UCSC browser shows relatively small exon blocks separated by longer connecting regions representing introns.

## 7. Interpretation
The analysis showed that MSH2 is a large gene located on chromosome 2 in the GRCh38/hg38 assembly, spanning approximately 306.8 kb. UCSC showed that the gene contains multiple transcripts and that the selected transcript, ENST00000233146.7, contains 16 exons separated by introns.

The UCSC annotation tracks also showed ClinVar variant information and differences in conservation across the MSH2 region. These annotations demonstrate that genomic databases can provide information about gene structure, evolutionary conservation, and clinically reported variants within the same genomic region.

The selected ClinVar variant, NM_000251.3(MSH2):c.942+3A>T, is located at chr2:47,414,421 in an intronic splice-donor region of MSH2. Although the variant is not located directly within a coding exon, its position near a splice site indicates that it can affect RNA processing. ClinVar reports evidence of exon 5 skipping, showing how an intronic variant can potentially alter the resulting MSH2 gene product.

Overall, the activity demonstrated how UCSC Genome Browser and NCBI ClinVar can be used together to connect a gene's genomic structure with a disease-associated variant and its reported clinical and molecular consequences.

### 2. Why is knowing the exact genomic location of a disease-associated variant useful?

Knowing the exact genomic location allows researchers to determine where the variant occurs relative to exons, introns, UTRs, and splice sites. It also makes it possible to accurately compare the variant with reference sequences and other genomic annotations.

### 3. What is one limitation of predicting a variant's effect only from its genomic location?

A genomic location alone does not always show how a variant affects the gene or protein. Experimental evidence, such as RNA splicing studies, protein-function studies, and clinical or family data, may be needed to determine its actual biological and disease-related effect.
